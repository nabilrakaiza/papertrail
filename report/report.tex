\section{Setup}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------

This report asks how four families of language model (an interpolated Kneser-Ney trigram, an RNN, an LSTM and a Transformer) compare at next-token prediction on WikiText-2 (Part~I); whether fixed pretrained input embeddings help or hurt compared with embeddings learned jointly with the model (Part~II); and whether a better language model also gives better representations for a downstream task, topic classification on AG News (Part~III).

\paragraph{Data.} WikiText-2~\cite{merity2016} (\tok{wikitext-2-v1}, the pre-tokenized release, via Hugging Face \tok{datasets}~\cite{lhoest2021}) with its official splits, lowercased, empty lines dropped and every line ended with \tok{<eos>}. The vocabulary comes from the training split only (28,913 types including \tok{<pad>}, \tok{<unk>} and \tok{<eos>}); other words become \tok{<unk>}. Each split is one token stream (2.08M training, 216K validation and 244K test tokens), read by the neural models in contiguous 64-token chunks.

\paragraph{Evaluation.} Every model is scored on the same 244,101 test targets (every test token except the first) by mean next-token cross-entropy; perplexity is its exponential. Padding, which appears only in the final chunk, is excluded. At evaluation the RNN and LSTM start every chunk from a zero state, so all neural models see at most 64 tokens of history; the trigram sees two. All results are from one seed (42) on an Apple M1 laptop (8 GB) using PyTorch 2.9~\cite{paszke2019} on the \tok{mps} device.

\section{Part I: Model families}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------

\subsection{Models}

\paragraph{Interpolated Kneser-Ney trigram.} An interpolated model mixes trigram, bigram and unigram estimates, and the lower orders matter most exactly when the trigram has no evidence. With ordinary interpolation the lower order is based on raw counts: a word's unigram probability is how often it occurs. That is misleading for a word that is frequent only because it follows one particular word: ``francisco'' is common, but almost always after ``san'', so it is a poor guess in a new context. Kneser-Ney smoothing~\cite{kneser1995,chen1999} instead bases the lower orders on continuation counts, the number of distinct words a word has been seen after, which better measures how likely it is to appear in a context it has not been seen in. We use absolute discounting with one fixed discount $D = 0.75$ at every order.

The order is three. A 4-gram model would not help on a corpus of this size because of data sparsity: on the training text, 85\% of the 1.38M distinct trigrams already occur exactly once, and for 4-grams, 1.81M of the 2.08M occurrences are distinct and 94\% of those occur once (\tok{results/analysis\_part1.json}). Most 4-gram contexts met at test time would therefore be unseen or rare, so the model would mostly fall back to its trigram estimate anyway.

\paragraph{Neural models.} An Elman RNN~\cite{mikolov2010}, an LSTM~\cite{hochreiter1997} and a decoder-only Transformer~\cite{vaswani2017} (Table~\ref{tab:arch}), each with randomly initialized, trainable 200-dimensional input embeddings (the size of the GloVe vectors used in Part~II) and a separate, untied output layer. All are trained alike: AdamW~\cite{loshchilov2019} (weight decay 0.01), batch size 32, 64-token chunks read in corpus order so that the RNN and LSTM carry their state across batches (truncated backpropagation through time), gradient clipping at 1.0, and at most 20 epochs with early stopping after 3 epochs without improvement, keeping the checkpoint with the lowest validation loss. The one difference is the learning rate: a constant $10^{-3}$ for the RNN and LSTM, and $5\times10^{-4}$ with 1,000 warm-up steps and cosine decay for the Transformer, since a post-LN Transformer tends to diverge without warm-up. Every model predicts each target from preceding tokens only; for each neural model we checked that changing one token leaves every earlier prediction unchanged.

\paragraph{Fairness.} The models come from different families (counting, recurrence and attention), so their sizes are not matched: each was sized to train in reasonable time on the laptop, and none was tuned. Everything around them is held equal: optimizer, epoch budget and early stopping, context length and evaluation.

\paragraph{Quality and cost.} The Transformer has the lowest test perplexity (149.52), followed by the LSTM (210.84), the trigram (226.00) and the RNN (245.14). It is also the cheapest neural model on every measure: size, time per epoch, time to its best checkpoint and inference speed. The RNN and LSTM cost the same per epoch; the LSTM reaches its best checkpoint in half the time because it needs fewer epochs. The trigram is fitted in seconds.

\paragraph{Learning behaviour.} In all three neural models training loss keeps falling while validation loss levels off (Figure~\ref{fig:part1curves}); training loss is measured with dropout on, so the true gap is larger than plotted. All three overfit. By the gap between training and validation loss the Transformer overfits most (0.78 nats at its best epoch, against 0.67 for the LSTM and 0.49 for the RNN); by how far validation loss rises after the best epoch the LSTM does (+0.088 nats, against +0.024 and +0.010), and early stopping keeps the checkpoint before the rise. The Transformer also has the lowest training loss at every epoch from the second on, while still reaching the lowest validation loss. One possible explanation is that attention gives it direct access to every earlier token in the window, while a recurrent model must squeeze its history into one fixed-size state, so the Transformer can fit the exact patterns of the training text more easily. The trigram has no curve, but it also memorizes: its perplexity on its own training text is 16.9, against 226.00 on test, and its discount plays the role of dropout and early stopping.

\paragraph{Generated text.} All models continue the same five prompts (temperature 0.8, top-$k$ 40, 50 new tokens; one sampling function, re-seeded for every continuation) (samples: \tok{results/generations.json}; examples in Appendix~\ref{app:samples}). The trigram's samples are locally fluent over two or three words but have no structure beyond that and drift off topic. The RNN's are more fluent locally but lose the thread within a sentence or two; the LSTM's hold together slightly better but still drift, most clearly for ``the species is found in''; the Transformer's are the most fluent, both locally and across sentences. On repetition, the trigram is the clearest case: two prompts ending in ``in'' led to identical continuations, since once both had sampled ``the'', its two-token context and the random draws coincided. Among the neural models the Transformer repeats most (share of distinct bigrams 0.90, against 0.93 to 0.94), for example ``766th'' three times in one sample, and the trigram produces the most \tok{<unk>} (6.2 per sample, against 3.0 to 4.0); with five samples per model, small differences mean little.

\subsection{How much history each model uses}

Figure~\ref{fig:byposition} splits the test loss by how many tokens of history each prediction had, that is, by position within the 64-token chunk (\tok{results/analysis\_part1.json}). With one token of history the RNN and LSTM are far worse than the Transformer and the trigram (perplexity 1,637 and 1,624, against 366 and 227). With more history all three neural models keep improving (from positions 8--15 to 32--63 their loss falls by 0.108, 0.181 and 0.132 nats) while the trigram stays flat; averaged over positions 32--63, the RNN is level with the trigram (224 against 225), the LSTM is at 184 and the Transformer at 138.

Two further protocols (Appendix~\ref{app:protocols}) agree: when no prediction is scored with fewer than 33 tokens of history, the RNN moves marginally ahead of the trigram (224.56 against 226.00) and the other rankings are unchanged; when the recurrent models carry their state across the whole test set, the LSTM improves by a further 18.5 perplexity points and the RNN by 3.2.

\subsection{Tradeoffs}

\paragraph{Observed.} The trigram is far cheaper than any neural model to fit and to score known text, but its perplexity is behind the LSTM's and the Transformer's (and ahead of the RNN's). The LSTM has slightly more parameters than the RNN (24.2M against 21.5M) and costs the same per epoch, but reaches a lower perplexity and gains much more from longer history (Table~\ref{tab:context}). The Transformer has the fewest parameters and the lowest perplexity, trains fastest and is the fastest at inference: among the neural models on this setup there is no tradeoff, it is better on every axis measured. This holds for what was tested: WikiText-2, models of the sizes in Table~\ref{tab:arch}, a laptop's training budget and a single seed.

\paragraph{Possible explanations.} The LSTM's advantage over the RNN is consistent with its gating, which lets gradients and information pass across many steps without vanishing; the history results fit a vanishing-gradient explanation for the RNN rather than the LSTM, since within the window the RNN gains least from extra history, and given unlimited history it improves by only 3.2 points against 18.5. The RNN's loss to the trigram is best explained by a cold start: during training the RNN and LSTM carried their state across batches and so almost never predicted from a zero state, which is how every evaluation chunk begins, while the stateless Transformer was trained and evaluated on chunks from scratch alike. A shortage of history alone does not explain it: with one token of history the Transformer (366) and the trigram (227) do far better. The Transformer's lead is consistent with its architecture: attention sees every earlier token in the window directly, instead of through a fixed-size recurrent state. Part of its lead comes from the chunk start, but not all of it: with that removed it is still well ahead (138.79 against 184.32 and 224.56). Its parameter count (the smallest) and context length (the same) do not explain it; its different learning-rate schedule cannot be ruled out.

\section{Part II: Embedding variants}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------

\subsection{Setup}

The Part~I LSTM is trained with three input-embedding settings; everything else (architecture, embedding size, data, training procedure and seed, and so every other initial weight) is identical. \emph{(1) Random, trainable:} the Part~I LSTM itself. \emph{(2) Own skip-gram, frozen:} 200-dimensional skip-gram vectors~\cite{mikolov2013} trained with gensim~\cite{rehurek2010} (window 5, 10 negative samples, 10 epochs) on the WikiText-2 training split only, covering every token except \tok{<pad>}; training them took 233\,s on the CPU, on top of the language-model training. \emph{(3) GloVe, frozen:} the public \tok{glove.6B.200d} vectors~\cite{pennington2014}, trained on 6B tokens of Wikipedia and Gigaword, covering 96.2\% of the vocabulary and 97.4\% of the training text; the most frequent missing tokens are \tok{<eos>} and the WikiText markers \tok{@-@}, \tok{@.@} and \tok{@,@}. Missing tokens get rows drawn from a normal distribution with mean 0 and the standard deviation of the found vectors (seeded), and the \emph{entire} table is frozen; after training it was checked to be bit-for-bit unchanged. Freezing leaves 18.4M trainable parameters instead of 24.2M.

Settings 1 and 2 learn from the same text and differ in whether the embeddings are learned jointly and kept trainable, or learned separately and frozen. Settings 2 and 3 are both learned separately and frozen and differ in the data (2M WikiText tokens against 6B tokens of general text), but also in the method, so they do not isolate the data alone.

\paragraph{Prediction quality.} Both frozen settings beat the random, trainable one by a wide margin: test perplexity 147.01 with the skip-gram vectors and 167.55 with GloVe, against 210.84. The frozen skip-gram LSTM is also slightly better than the Part~I Transformer (149.52).

\paragraph{Convergence and stability.} After one epoch the three settings are level (validation loss 5.91, 5.93 and 5.99). From the second epoch on both frozen settings are lower at every epoch and keep improving for longer: their best epoch is 10, against 7 for the random setting, whose validation loss then rises (Figure~\ref{fig:part2curves}). All curves are smooth after the first epoch; the random setting's best epoch is a small isolated dip. Time per epoch is similar (452 and 433\,s against 480\,s, within the variation between timing rounds).

\paragraph{Generated text.} With the same prompts and decoding as in Part~I (\tok{results/generations\_part2.json}), the random setting's samples tend to drift off topic, while both frozen settings stay on topic more consistently (``the species is found in'' continues with genus and habitats under skip-gram, and with birds under GloVe) and are more fluent locally. They also repeat more within a sample (share of distinct bigrams 0.92 and 0.91, against 0.94; the skip-gram species sample uses ``species'' four times), and GloVe produces the most \tok{<unk>} (5.2 per sample, against 2.0 and 3.0).

\subsection{Interpretation}

\paragraph{Trainability (settings 1 and 2).} Learning the embeddings separately and then freezing them helps here. One possible reason is stability: with a frozen table the LSTM's inputs stop moving, while in the random setting the table and the rest of the model must be learned at once from the same data, so the LSTM is fitted to inputs that keep moving. The frozen settings also overfit later and less (their best epoch comes three epochs later, at a lower validation loss). A second, simpler reason may contribute: freezing leaves 5.8M fewer parameters to fit from the same 2.08M training tokens, so less room to memorize the training text. The two are compatible, and this experiment cannot separate them.

\paragraph{Extra data (settings 2 and 3).} The skip-gram vectors were trained on the very corpus the language model is trained and tested on, so they match its vocabulary, tokenization and topics; GloVe was trained on different, differently tokenized text. On this corpus that match appears to outweigh GloVe's roughly 3,000 times more data, although the two settings also differ in method. It shows in coverage too: GloVe lacks \tok{<eos>} and the \tok{@-@}, \tok{@.@} and \tok{@,@} markers, among the most frequent tokens here, which therefore keep random, frozen rows. On text closer to GloVe's own we would expect GloVe to do better; that was not tested.

\paragraph{The same embeddings on the Transformer.} Applied to the Transformer as an optional extension (Appendix~\ref{app:transformer}), with the vectors rescaled to its own initial scale, frozen pretrained embeddings help as well: test perplexity 131.17 with skip-gram and 127.45 with GloVe, against 149.52. Here GloVe beats skip-gram, the reverse of the LSTM result. One possible reason is that the difference between the two sets of vectors reflects their hyperparameters (dimension, window, training data) more than the algorithms themselves, in line with Levy et al.~\cite{levy2015}, so which set wins need not be stable from one model to another.

\paragraph{A confound: scale.} The three tables also start at different scales: the random table has standard deviation 1.0 (PyTorch's default), the skip-gram vectors 0.19 and GloVe 0.43. The initial scale matters in itself, so part of the gap between the random setting and the frozen ones may come from scale rather than from what the vectors contain. This experiment cannot separate the two; a random, trainable table started at the pretrained vectors' scale would.

\section{Part III: Downstream representations}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------

\subsection{Setup}

\paragraph{Task and data.} Topic classification on AG News~\cite{zhang2015} (World, Sports, Business, Sci/Tech; balanced, so chance is 25\%). A class-balanced, seeded sample of 10,000 items from the official training split is used for training and a disjoint 2,000 for validation; the official test split (7,600) is used once. Items are lowercased, tokenized following WikiText-2's conventions and mapped to its vocabulary (7.0\% of test tokens become \tok{<unk>}), and cut to the 64-token training length (3.8\% of test items are longer).

\paragraph{Representations and classifier.} Six frozen language models are probed: the Part~I LSTM and Transformer and the four frozen-embedding variants (Part~II and Appendix~\ref{app:transformer}). Each reads every item from a fresh state, and its final-layer hidden states (512 numbers per token for the LSTMs, 200 for the Transformers) become one vector per item by \emph{mean pooling} over the real tokens, excluding padding, or by \emph{last-token pooling}, the state at the last real token, which has read the whole item; padding comes after the item, so it cannot affect these states. The classifier is the same for all twelve: multinomial logistic regression (scikit-learn~\cite{pedregosa2011}) on standardized features (fitted on the training items only), with the regularization strength chosen by validation macro-F1 from $\{0.01, 0.1, 1, 10\}$. Only the classifier is trained.

\paragraph{Which representation works better.} Mean pooling beats last-token pooling for all six models, most for the Part~I LSTM (15.3 points) and least for the frozen-embedding Transformers (1.4 to 1.8). With the embeddings held fixed, the Transformer beats the LSTM every time, under both poolings, and frozen pretrained embeddings raise accuracy sharply: the LSTM goes from 62.89\% to 79.39\% with skip-gram and 83.18\% with GloVe, and the best representation overall is the Transformer with GloVe (85.64\%). For 84\% of test items the last real token is a full stop, so last-token pooling takes the state just after a sentence ends, whose job is mostly to predict what follows the end of a sentence rather than to describe the item; the topic is spread over the item, and averaging keeps it. The pooling gap also fits the architectural difference seen in Part~I: in the LSTM, early information must pass through every later step to reach the final state, while the Transformer's last position attends to every earlier token directly. Likewise, each LSTM state compresses all earlier history into one fixed-size state, while each Transformer state draws on the earlier tokens directly.

\paragraph{Errors.} Across the four probes of the Part~I models, Sports is easiest and Business and Sci/Tech hardest (best probe: F1 0.89 for Sports, 0.80 World, 0.73 Sci/Tech, 0.71 Business), and the largest confusions are between Business and Sci/Tech (331 and 320 items). The most confident errors (Appendix~\ref{app:errors}) include sports stories labelled World, arguably labelling problems; items that fit both Business and Sci/Tech; and a few plain model errors. Unknown words give the Business and Sci/Tech confusion only weak support (Appendix~\ref{app:errors}). A more likely explanation is ambiguity: much news, such as a technology company's earnings, is as much Business as Sci/Tech, and since each item carries a single hard label rather than a distribution over classes, such items will be confused whichever representation is used.

\paragraph{Language-model ranking against downstream ranking.} Between the two Part~I models the rankings agree. Across all six they mostly agree, but not fully: the Spearman rank correlation between test perplexity and test accuracy is 0.66 with mean pooling and 0.83 with last-token pooling. The clearest exception is the LSTM with frozen GloVe, fifth of six in perplexity (167.55) but second in classification with mean pooling (83.18\%); GloVe gives the better features for both architectures. Better next-token prediction goes with better features in general here, but the pretrained vectors a model starts from can matter more downstream than its perplexity suggests; six models are still a small sample. A possible reason, offered as a hypothesis: GloVe's training text includes the Gigaword newswire corpus, and AG News is news, so its vectors may suit this task better than vectors trained on Wikipedia text alone.

\section{Limitations and lessons}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
\paragraph{Limitations.} The main limitation is compute: everything ran on one laptop (Apple M1, 8\,GB, no CUDA GPU), so the models are small and untuned, each run had a limited budget, and larger models are untested. The rest follow from it. \emph{One seed:} there are no error bars, so small differences may be run-to-run variation. \emph{Protocol:} resetting the recurrent state every 64 tokens caps the RNN's and LSTM's history and penalizes the start of each chunk, so the headline numbers understate them. \emph{Confounds:} in Part~II the tables differ in scale as well as content, and skip-gram against GloVe changes the method as well as the data; Part~III's tokenizer only approximates WikiText-2's and loses 7\% of AG News tokens to \tok{<unk>}, and six models are few for a correlation. \emph{Transformer extension:} both runs hit the 20-epoch limit while still improving, and they rely on rescaling the vectors. \emph{Timing:} plugged in (Low Power Mode), a training step takes 1.7 times as long as on battery, so all costs were measured plugged in.

\paragraph{Failure cases.} The RNN scores worse than the trigram under the standard protocol, and the evidence points to the start of each chunk rather than to long-range forgetting. The first LSTM run was invalid: in PyTorch 2.9 on \tok{mps}, the built-in two-layer LSTM's inter-layer dropout keeps the mode of its first call and a fixed mask, caught only because a reloaded checkpoint did not reproduce its recorded loss. In Part~III many confident errors are doubtful or ambiguous labels.

\paragraph{Tool use.} An AI coding assistant was used substantially for the code and for drafting this report from the author's notes; Appendix~\ref{app:tools} states exactly how.

\paragraph{Lessons learned.} (1) How Kneser-Ney smoothing works, and why continuation counts are a better lower-order estimate than raw frequency. (2) A neural model is not automatically better than a well-smoothed n-gram: the RNN lost to the trigram here. (3) The evaluation protocol can decide a ranking: the RNN's loss came from a cold start that only a per-position breakdown revealed. (4) Results need checking, not just running: reloading each checkpoint exposed the LSTM dropout bug.

\subsection{Other evaluation protocols (Part I)}\label{app:protocols}
\paragraph{Other ways of giving the models history.} Table~\ref{tab:context} scores the same test targets under two further protocols (\tok{results/analysis\_part1.json}). \emph{Strided}: windows of 64 tokens start every 32 tokens, each from a fresh state, and only their second half is scored, so every scored prediction has between 33 and 64 tokens of history and no model's slow start is counted. \emph{Carried}: the RNN and LSTM keep their state across the whole test stream, as in training, so their history is unlimited; the Transformer cannot do this, because it only ever attends to a fixed window.

\begin{table}[h]
\centering\small
\begin{tabular}{lrrr}
\toprule
Model & Standard (64-token chunks) & Strided (33--64 tokens of history) & Carried (unlimited history) \\
\midrule
KN trigram & 226.00 & 226.00 & 226.00 \\
RNN & 245.14 & 224.56 & 221.35 \\
LSTM & 210.84 & 184.32 & 165.82 \\
Transformer & 149.52 & 138.79 & -- \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{Test perplexity of the same checkpoints under three evaluation protocols. The trigram always conditions on two tokens, so its score does not change.}
\label{tab:context}
\end{table}

With the slow start removed (strided), the RNN moves from behind the trigram to marginally ahead of it (224.56 against 226.00), and the ranking of the other models is unchanged. Given unlimited history (carried), the LSTM improves by a further 18.5 perplexity points, the RNN by 3.2. The Transformer keeps improving up to the end of its 64-token window; how it would behave beyond that was not measured. The standard protocol, fixed in the README in advance, remains the headline comparison; these are extra evidence.

\subsection{The Part II embeddings on the Transformer}\label{app:transformer}
As an optional extension, the Part~I Transformer was also trained with the skip-gram and GloVe tables frozen in (\tok{results/transformer\_*\_frozen.json}). Its embeddings are multiplied by $\sqrt{200} \approx 14$ before the position signal is added, and it is built for a table at its own initial scale (standard deviation 0.02), so both tables were rescaled by a single constant to that scale, which keeps every vector's direction and the ratios of the distances between them. Everything else matches the Part~I Transformer, which leaves 7.7M trainable parameters instead of 13.5M.

\subsection{Representative errors and unknown words (Part III)}\label{app:errors}
The ten most confident errors of each probe include three kinds:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Doubtful labels.} Sports stories labelled World are the most common confident error in every probe, for example ``Football: Azerbaijan 0-1 England. Michael Owen heads England's winner in the World Cup qualifier'' (labelled World, predicted Sports). These are arguably problems of the dataset, not of the model.
\item \emph{Genuinely ambiguous topics.} ``How Serious Is Pfizer's Condition? The stock is feeling some pain these days'' (Business, predicted Sci/Tech) and ``Treo 650 on Monday? The blogs are buzzing that the Treo 650 will be released'' (Sci/Tech, predicted Business) could each reasonably belong to either class.
\item \emph{Model errors.} ``Calif.\ Lawmaker Wants to Privatize State Pensions'' (Business) is predicted as Sports with full confidence by the Transformer with last-token pooling.
\end{itemize}

One possible contributor to the Business and Sci/Tech confusion is unknown words, since both classes are full of company and product names that WikiText-2 does not contain. The data give this only weak support: Sci/Tech has the highest \tok{<unk>} rate (8.2\% of tokens, against 6.2\% for World), but Business (7.0\%) is no higher than Sports (7.0\%); in the best probe, Business items predicted as Sci/Tech contain slightly more \tok{<unk>} than correctly classified ones (7.8\% against 7.0\%), and Sci/Tech items predicted as Business hardly more (8.4\% against 8.3\%); in the two LSTM probes, Sci/Tech items predicted as Business contain \emph{fewer} \tok{<unk>} than correctly classified ones.
